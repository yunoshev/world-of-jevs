# Writing a creature card

A World of Jevs card describes one creature type in its local setting. It should help a reader understand who the creature is, what it can do, and what happens around it. It is a description of intended behavior, not proof that every scene has been tested.

## What to record

- **Identity and role:** The creature's name, type, location, and what it guards, pursues, or supports.
- **Local goal:** What it is trying to accomplish here, in terms it could know from its surroundings.
- **Relationships:** Nearby allies and enemies, including whom it assists or calls for help.
- **Abilities:** Each known action or spell, its intended target, the condition that makes it available, and its observed timing in seconds.
- **Ordinary life:** Whether it stands guard, patrols, wanders, follows, or returns home after an interruption.
- **Combat reactions:** How it chooses targets, changes behavior at low health, flees, summons, or enters a new phase.
- **Speech and sound:** When it speaks or makes a sound and whether the exact line is known. If nothing is established, say “not documented.”
- **Consequences:** What changes when it dies or an encounter ends, such as a door, summon, or next phase.
- **Limits:** What the creature cannot see, know, say, or do; do not invent abilities or unseen events.

Keep observations separate from interpretation. A source-confirmed action, a behavior seen in a test, and a plausible guess are different claims. If a detail is unknown, leave it unknown instead of filling it with lore.

## Small fictional example

> **Workshop Gatekeeper** — A sentry protecting a workshop entrance. Holds position with two nearby sentries and challenges visible intruders. Can strike a nearby enemy after a short opening delay; if wounded, may call the sentries for help. Returns to the gate when the threat is gone. No specific spoken line is documented. Cannot know about intruders beyond its visible surroundings.

For a complex creature, add a short table of abilities and a sequence of phase changes. Keep timings and triggers concrete enough to compare with what a player actually sees. A card may describe a single encounter without claiming that the entire dungeon has been accepted.
